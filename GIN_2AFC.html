⚠ Before you start: check your audio setup

  • Use wired headphones — not Bluetooth. Bluetooth compresses the audio (SBC/AAC codecs), which can smear or fill short gaps. Noise cancelling and automatic gain change what reaches your ear. A headset whose microphone is in use drops to a low-quality "hands-free" mode that removes high frequencies. Power-saving can cut off the first part of each sound.
  • Turn off sound processing. Windows: Settings › Sound › your device › Audio enhancements Off, Spatial sound Off, and no Loudness Equalization, Dolby, Nahimic or Realtek effects. macOS/iOS: Spatial Audio and EQ off. Phones: Dolby / EQ / "adapt sound" off. Compressors and loudness equalizers can fill in gaps and change the level of tones.
  • Close other audio apps, and leave the system volume fixed for the whole test — adjust level only with the slider below.

Why the target % matters in a 2-interval task

In a two-interval forced-choice task you are right half the time by guessing alone, so performance runs from 50% (hear nothing) to 100% (hear it every time), not from 0%.

  • Where each rule settles. An N-down/1-up staircase settles where stepping down and stepping up are equally likely: pN = 0.5, so p = 0.51/N. That gives 50% (1-down), 70.7% (2-down) and 79.4% (3-down) correct (Levitt, 1971).
  • Correcting for guessing. If a listener truly hears the difference on a proportion p′ of trials and guesses on the rest, then p = p′ + 0.5(1 − p′), so p′ = (p − 0.5) / 0.5. The 79.4% target is only ≈59% true detection; 70.7% is ≈41%; and 50% is 0% — pure guessing.
  • Why 1-down/1-up fails here. Its 50% target is exactly what a listener who hears nothing scores, so the staircase keeps stepping toward the smallest difference. In a yes/no task (like the single-interval GIN page) 50% "yes" is a real detection point, because guessing there is not fixed at 50%.
  • Signal-detection view. The guessing correction assumes "high-threshold" theory. Signal detection theory describes the same targets as sensitivity d′ = √2·z(p): about 0.78 at 70.7% and 1.16 at 79.4%.

About This Procedure

Task & stimulus (gap detection)

  • Each trial presents two 1-second broadband noise intervals, played back to back. Exactly one interval contains a brief silent gap; the other is continuous noise. Which interval (first or second) contains the gap is randomized each trial.
  • Noise is freshly generated per interval per trial, so no waveform repeats.
  • Gap onset/offset use a short (~5ms, or half the gap for very short gaps) linear fade rather than an instant cut, to avoid an audible "click" at the edges that could act as a cue independent of the gap itself.
  • The gap's position within its interval is randomized per trial (kept at least 100ms from the interval's start/end — i.e. somewhere in the middle 800ms of each 1000ms interval), rather than fixed at the interval's midpoint, so its timing can't be anticipated.

Task & stimulus (frequency discrimination)

  • Each trial presents two 400 ms pure tones separated by 500 ms of silence. One is the standard (the frequency you chose); the other is the comparison, higher by Δf. Which interval holds the higher tone is randomized each trial, and you pick the higher one.
  • Level rove. Each tone's level is randomly varied by up to ±3 dB. Headphones do not have a flat frequency response, so the higher tone may come out slightly louder or softer. Without the rove, a listener could learn "louder means higher" and answer from loudness instead of pitch. The rove makes loudness an unreliable cue.
  • Smooth on/off ramps. Each tone fades in and out over 20 ms with a raised-cosine (smooth S-shaped) ramp. An abrupt start or a short linear ramp spreads energy to other frequencies ("spectral splatter"), heard as a click that could itself be a cue.
  • The standard stays fixed and the comparison is always higher. A listener could in principle compare each tone with a remembered standard; research versions often also rove the standard frequency from trial to trial to prevent this.

Forced choice

  • You must pick First or Second every trial, even if you're not sure. Forcing a guess (rather than offering an "unsure" option) is what makes this design bias-free — it removes the effect of individual differences in willingness to commit to an answer, so the measured threshold reflects genuine sensitivity rather than response caution.
  • The price is a 50% guessing floor; see "Why the target % matters" in the Setup card.

Channel routing (ear selection)

  • The Channel selector routes sound to Binaural (both ears), Left Ear Only, or Right Ear Only via a true stereo buffer — the non-selected ear receives silence, not just a quieter signal.
  • Changing the channel mid-session requires re-confirming calibration before the test can continue.

Audio hardware & volume calibration

  • The audio-setup checklist must be ticked before calibration can be confirmed. A web browser cannot reliably tell whether the output is Bluetooth or whether the sound card is processing the audio, so this relies on you checking.
  • Before any scored trial, you must play the calibration sound (noise for gap detection; a pulsed tone at the standard frequency for frequency discrimination), adjust the volume slider to a comfortable level, and confirm it sounds correct for the selected channel.
  • Play Trial and the response buttons are disabled (gated) until calibration is confirmed.
  • This standardizes relative comfort and verifies correct ear routing — it does not measure absolute sound pressure level (dB SPL), which depends on your specific headphones/speakers and device volume.

Practice block

  • Immediately after calibration, 3 unscored practice trials run using an easy, large difference (a 20 ms gap, or a 30% frequency difference) so you can get familiar with the two-interval task before anything counts.

Staircase & threshold estimation

  • An N-down/1-up staircase: the difference only shrinks (harder) after N consecutive correct responses; a single incorrect response (including a wrong guess) immediately grows it (easier) and resets the correct streak. The choice of N sets the accuracy level the staircase converges on (Levitt, 1971):
    • 3-down/1-up → ~79.4% correct (≈59% after correcting for guessing). The most conservative (largest) threshold, and the longest test.
    • 2-down/1-up → ~70.7% correct (≈41% after correcting for guessing). A common middle ground: a slightly smaller threshold and a shorter test.
    • 1-down/1-up → 50% correct. In a two-interval task, guessing alone already scores 50%, so this rule tracks chance performance. It drifts toward the smallest differences, and its "threshold" does not measure discrimination. It is included only to show why it is unsuitable for a forced-choice task.
  • Selected for this session: 3-down/1-up. The rule is locked once calibration is confirmed.
  • If the staircase is already at its easiest or hardest level, it cannot move, so no reversal is counted on that trial.
  • The gap steps one value at a time through a fixed list (0–20 ms).
  • The test stops after 8 reversals (points where the staircase's direction flips).
  • Your threshold is estimated as the mean gap duration at the last 6 reversals — the first 2 are discarded, since they reflect the staircase still settling from its starting point rather than steady-state performance.

Reference values

  • Musiek et al. (2005), the clinical GIN test, 50 normal-hearing listeners: mean approximate gap threshold 4.8 ms (left ear) and 4.9 ms (right ear). Ear and Hearing 26(6):608–18, doi:10.1097/01.aud.0000188069.80699.41.
  • That test is a different procedure (single-interval, calibrated clinical recordings, a different threshold rule), so compare your result only loosely. These are not clinical norms for this page.
  • Multiplicative steps. Δf starts at 20% of the standard and is divided (harder) or multiplied (easier) by a step factor, rather than changed by a fixed number of Hz. Frequency discrimination roughly follows Weber's law (Δf grows with f), so proportional steps are equal-sized on the scale that matters, at any standard frequency.
  • Big steps, then small. The factor is ×2 until the 4th reversal, then ×√2 (≈1.41). Big steps get from the starting point to the threshold region quickly; smaller steps then give a finer estimate.
  • Δf is limited to 0.05%–50% of the standard.
  • The test stops after 12 reversals. The first 4 (made with the big step) are discarded, and the threshold is the geometric mean of the last 8. A geometric mean is used because the steps are proportional (equal on a log scale).
  • The result is reported as Δf in Hz and as the Weber fraction Δf/f (shown as a %).
